\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The results illustrate why high-frequency digital trace data can add information beyond aggregate estimates of shutdown costs. A national GDP series cannot show whether digital workers cease production, shift to offline tasks, or accumulate work that is released later. GitHub timestamps reveal all three margins imperfectly but at daily and commit-level frequency.

The main estimate---an 86.8 percent decline in the daily active-user rate relative to the synthetic counterfactual (\cref{tab:sc_main})---is best interpreted as a collapse in observable online software-production activity. The mechanism shows a small observed backlog alongside a collapse in author-time levels: creation of code later observed publicly falls roughly 96--97 percent from the pre-shutdown comparison, and backlog represents about 2.5 percent of normal volume. The original 28.27 percent backlog share conditions on a much smaller calendar-cohort denominator. It does not establish that missing activity was displaced across time, or quantify destruction of work that never became public.

At the same time, delayed synchronization cannot account for the entire collapse. Most eligible users record no productive public activity during the shutdown, and the recovery backlog is much smaller than the counterfactual volume implied by the active-rate and shipping estimates. Connectivity therefore appears to matter not only for publishing already-written code but also for complementary tasks: accessing remote dependencies, coordinating with collaborators, reviewing code, resolving issues, merging pull requests, and releasing software. The especially large declines in shipping and collaboration are consistent with these channels.

The sectoral results add a dynamic dimension. In aggregate, Bangladesh's gap relative to the donor mean returned to within its pre-shutdown range in the first week after restoration (\cref{fig:did_es}). Reconnection did not restore observable activity at once: weeks after the shutdown, activity in Education-, Health-, and especially Accommodation/Food-related repositories remained below its pre-shutdown level, while Finance- and Information-related activity recovered within about three weeks. This pattern is consistent with the costs of a shutdown extending beyond the days without connectivity and falling unevenly across types of projects. The weaker recovery of pre-existing repositories, relative to sector-wide activity, further suggests that part of the recovery took the form of new projects or switching across repositories, which an analysis restricted to pre-existing projects would miss. Because the sectoral analysis has no untreated comparison group and classifies repositories rather than workers, these interpretations remain suggestive.

These results are relevant for firms and policymakers in digitally intensive economies. Resilience planning that focuses only on local editing tools may be insufficient because modern software production depends on a broader stack of remote services. Local mirrors of repositories and package registries, redundant international connectivity, offline-capable documentation, and asynchronous development workflows may mitigate some losses, but they cannot fully substitute for network access when teams, deployment infrastructure, and customers remain online.
