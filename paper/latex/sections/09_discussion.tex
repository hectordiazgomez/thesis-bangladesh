\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The results illustrate why high-frequency digital trace data can add information beyond aggregate estimates of shutdown costs. A national GDP series cannot show whether digital workers cease production, shift to offline tasks, or accumulate work that is released later. GitHub timestamps reveal all three margins imperfectly but at daily and commit-level frequency.

The main estimate---an 86.8 percent decline in the daily active-user rate relative to the synthetic counterfactual---is best interpreted as a collapse in observable online software-production activity. The mechanism results indicate that some of this activity is displaced across time rather than destroyed. At least within the strict public-commit sample, roughly one-quarter to one-third of commits authored during the outage appear only after the main shutdown window. This implies that treating observed pushes as identical to contemporaneous production would overstate the immediate loss in underlying coding effort.

At the same time, delayed synchronization cannot account for the entire collapse. Most eligible users record no productive public activity during the shutdown, and the recovery backlog is much smaller than the counterfactual volume implied by the active-rate and shipping estimates. Connectivity therefore appears to matter not only for publishing already-written code but also for complementary tasks: accessing remote dependencies, coordinating with collaborators, reviewing code, resolving issues, merging pull requests, and releasing software. The especially large declines in shipping and collaboration are consistent with these channels.

These results are relevant for firms and policymakers in digitally intensive economies. Resilience planning that focuses only on local editing tools may be insufficient because modern software production depends on a broader stack of remote services. Local mirrors of repositories and package registries, redundant international connectivity, offline-capable documentation, and asynchronous development workflows may mitigate some losses, but they cannot fully substitute for network access when teams, deployment infrastructure, and customers remain online.
