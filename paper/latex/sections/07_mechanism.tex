\section{Mechanism: Offline Work and Delayed Synchronization}
\label{sec:mechanism}

\subsection{Main backlog measure}
\label{sec:backlog}

A fall in public GitHub events does not necessarily imply an equal fall in code creation. Git is decentralized: developers may continue editing files and creating commits locally, then push them only after connectivity returns. The commit-level analysis examines this pattern among code that is subsequently observed publicly.

In the strict sample, 2,932 commits are authored during July 18--23. Of these, 829 first appear in a public push on or after July 24. The resulting author-time backlog share is 28.27 percent (\cref{tab:mechanism}). Replacing the author timestamp with the committer timestamp produces a nearly identical 27.16 percent backlog share (790 of 2,909 commits). The two timestamps do not validate each other: \texttt{authoredDate} and \texttt{committedDate} are Git metadata that can be set by the same local client and clock, so their agreement shows only that the result is not sensitive to the choice of field. Neither is an externally verified record of when the code was written. The mechanism evidence is therefore consistent with local work followed by delayed synchronization, but it does not directly observe the underlying production process.

Comparisons in levels sharply limit how much adaptation this backlog represents. The calendar cohort contains 2,932 commits (835 users), including 1,835 timestamped before 15:00 UTC on July 18, approximately when national connectivity collapsed \citep{belson2024}. Starting the author-time cohort at that onset leaves 1,097 commits from 250 users; 752 were first pushed on or after July 24 (68.55 percent), compared with 829 of 2,932 (28.27 percent) in the original calendar cohort. Both definitions retain pushes observed through August 10.

Inverse push-day sampling weights produce a mean of 33,787.50 commits across the six original six-day placebo windows. The onset cohort is 96.75 percent smaller, but lasts 5.375 rather than six days. Matching the placebo windows to the same 15:00 start gives a baseline of 30,038.26 and a 96.35 percent decline; scaling the six-day baseline by duration gives 96.38 percent. Creation of code that became public by August 10 therefore fell roughly 96--97 percent, and delayed publication within that horizon offsets only a small part of the shortfall. The data cannot determine how much of the remaining shortfall reflects work that was not done, work pushed after August 10, or work never published. The original calendar cohort falls 91.32 percent relative to its six-day baseline. Backlog is small in levels: 829 commits are 2.45 percent of the six-day baseline, and the onset cohort's 752 are 2.50 percent of the matched baseline. These are descriptive level comparisons, not a second causal estimate; the early-July activity increase and unresolved metadata both affect their interpretation.

The 345 onset-cohort commits pushed before July 24 involve 64 users. Of these, 246 were pushed on July 23, when fixed broadband began returning; 99 were pushed earlier. The calendar window therefore cannot be treated as complete isolation for every sampled account. Possible explanations include physical location outside Bangladesh, exceptional access, timestamp error and automated publication. The case audit below directly documents the last mechanism for some residual activity. Nor is observation complete: 35 calendar-cohort backlog commits first appear on August 5--10. Further pushes after August 10 and code never published are censored. The estimated creation decline therefore applies to code eventually observed publicly within this horizon, not all code written.

The endpoint also matters. Cloudflare's August account places the beginning of fixed-broadband recovery at 13:00 UTC on July 23, whereas its quarterly summary gives 12:00 UTC \citep{belson2024,belson2024q3}. Using 12:00 and 13:00 UTC as alternative endpoints yields 899 and 904 onset-authored commits, respectively, from 211 accounts under either definition, compared with 1,097 from 250 accounts through the end of July 23. Of these narrower cohorts, 123 commits were pushed before their respective endpoint; 674 and 678 were first pushed from July 24 onward. These are alternative descriptive counts with different exposure durations, not revised level-effect estimates. The original six-day treatment window and the previously reported onset cohort remain unchanged.

Long publication delays are far more common during the shutdown than before it. Among shutdown-authored commits, 29.71 percent have an author-to-push delay above 24 hours, 25.38 percent above 48 hours, and 22.75 percent above 72 hours. In six overlapping pre-shutdown placebo windows, the corresponding averages are approximately 1.10 percent, 0.72 percent, and 0.46 percent (\cref{tab:mech_placebos}). Because commit metadata were queried for only a daily sample of commits pushed before July 18 but for all commits pushed from July 18 onward, the placebo rates use inverse-probability weights, while shutdown-authored commits, all pushed on or after July 18, receive weight one. The shutdown cohort therefore lies far outside the normal pre-period delay distribution.

\subsection{Timing and concentration of delayed pushes}
\label{sec:timing}

The delayed commits are released quickly once connectivity begins to return (\cref{fig:mechanism}). Of the 829 author-time backlog commits, 354 (42.7 percent) appear on July 24 and 544 (65.6 percent) by July 25. By July 27, 81.1 percent have appeared; by July 31, 92.0 percent have appeared. This temporal concentration immediately after restoration is consistent with a synchronization backlog rather than ordinary long-lived branches or sporadic publication.

The backlog is not generated by only a handful of unusually active users (\cref{tab:concentration}). The 2,932 shutdown-authored commits come from 835 users and 977 repositories. Two hundred twenty-two users (26.6 percent) and 246 repositories (25.2 percent) contribute at least one backlog commit. The largest user accounts for 2.77 percent of backlog commits, the top five users for 12.06 percent, and the top ten for 21.47 percent. Dropping the twenty users with the most backlog still leaves a 20.37 percent backlog share.

Backlog is, however, more common in larger pushes (\cref{tab:push_size}). Restricting to pushes with at most 10 commits lowers the backlog share to 21.0 percent; at most 5 commits, 14.4 percent; at most 2 commits, 7.8 percent; and single-commit pushes, 3.3 percent. This pattern is economically meaningful rather than merely inconvenient: if developers accumulate local commits while offline and synchronize later, restoration should produce larger-than-normal push batches. At the same time, it means that the 28.3 percent calendar-cohort backlog share should not be interpreted as applying uniformly to every kind of push.


\subsection{Who remains observable during the outage?}
\label{sec:residual_users}

Publication during a calendar shutdown window is not the same as coding without connectivity. Among the 10,241 eligible Bangladesh-associated accounts, 1,128 publish a primary event at least once on July 18--23, but only 40 (0.39 percent) do so on July 19--22, the interior days that exclude both the partial first day and the beginning of broadband restoration. The denominator is users rather than user-days: this is the share active at least once across four days, not a daily activity rate. The strict onset-author cohort contains 250 accounts (2.44 percent); 191 publish at least one onset-authored commit from July 24 onward. Only 15 of the 250 also belong to the 40-account core publication group. Public posting and timestamped local authorship thus select substantially different groups.

\Cref{tab:advisor_residual} characterizes them using behavior observed before the shutdown. Core publishers were active on 36.76 percent of pre-period days on average, versus 17.18 percent for other eligible users; their median pre-period number of pushes was 47.5 versus 21. Onset authors were active on 32.62 percent of pre-period days and made a median of 51.5 pushes. Delayed onset authors had a similar activity rate of 32.27 percent. Residual visibility is consequently concentrated among accounts that were already more active, rather than a representative random subset of eligible accounts. Collaboration remains uncommon in all groups; its mean daily incidence is 0.99 percent for core publishers and 0.35 percent for other accounts. These differences describe selection into observed activity, not an effect of personal characteristics on resilience. Among users with pre-period pushes, the mean share of pushes to repositories owned by the same account is 91.21 percent for core publishers and 93.19 percent for other eligible users. This ownership match characterizes their public project activity; it does not identify whether the work was personal, educational or performed for an employer.

Public account-creation dates provide a complementary historical characteristic. Among the 37 core publishers whose current account object resolves and is consistent with their observed 2024 history, median account age at shutdown was 6.28 years; it was 2.95 years among 9,723 other eligible accounts with consistent dates. Onset authors were different: their median age was 3.05 years among 249 consistent records. Thus the accounts still publishing in the interior days were older and more active, whereas the author-time group was mainly distinguished by higher prior activity. Account creation is not a measure of age, professional experience or employment. The metadata were retrieved in October 2026; 439 eligible account objects were unavailable and 42 creation dates conflicted with the historical event record, potentially reflecting changed or reused logins. Those records are excluded from account-age summaries rather than assigned a historical age. Repository-domain composition is reported in \cref{tab:residual_domains}, retaining the unclassified group rather than inferring occupations from sparse labels. In particular, 32 of the 40 core publishers and 192 of the 250 onset authors remain unclassified; the small labelled cells cannot establish a distinctive occupational composition. The core group's 237 primary events are also concentrated: the largest account contributes 17.72 percent and the ten largest 60.34 percent. This concentration describes residual publication, a different quantity from the broader delayed-commit backlog.

There are 19 strict onset authors who publish before July 23, accounting for the 99 commits discussed above; another 48 publish on July 23, with some overlap. Their timestamps do not identify whether they were abroad, had exceptional access, or used an incorrectly set clock. I therefore avoid inferring occupation, employer, wealth, political affiliation or physical residence from continued posting. The mechanism cohort additionally requires eventual public publication and metadata resolution by August 10. More active users are more likely to satisfy those observation conditions, and people who continued working but never published remain absent.

\subsection{Automation and the content of residual activity}
\label{sec:automation_audit}

An individual case review examines the union of the two cohorts: 275 accounts, 1,232 distinct repository--commit pairs and 321 repository snapshots. Public evidence retrieved in October 2026 resolves 1,210 commit objects and 314 repositories. The review reads author and committer identities, commit messages, available workflows at exact historical commit hashes, and changed-file lists for all 165 retrievable core push heads. Current profile labels are not treated as historical residence or proof of manual operation. Research on repository bots motivates combining identity, message and change evidence rather than relying on usernames alone \citep{dey2020bots,golzadeh2021bots}. This is an AI-assisted case review with a documented rubric, not a validated classifier or an independently replicated human annotation exercise. The complete case register, evidence URLs and retrieval dates are supplied with the replication files; \cref{tab:evidence_cases} reports every core publisher.

Direct evidence of automated publication is present in 113 of the 180 core-window PushEvents (62.8 percent), involving 18 of the 40 accounts. Their heads comprise 55 dependency updates, 31 generated profile graphics, 15 profile-metric updates, eight deployments and four generated-data updates. Machine author or committer identities are corroborated by the messages and changed files; historical scheduled workflows provide additional evidence for the profile-metric and generated-data cases. GitHub workflows can run on schedules or remote events \citep{githubactions}; a resulting push need not require a new connection from the person associated with the repository. In particular, the account with 42 primary events, 17.72 percent of the residual total, has 41 push heads attributed to a dependency-update bot. The remaining event is retained. A normal account type therefore does not imply manual publication.

The audit also retains uncertainty. Four host-list pushes show highly regular timing and small repeated changes. An update-and-push script and example crontab exist, but deployment of that schedule is unverified; these pushes remain suggestive only. Fifteen heads cannot be recovered. The remaining 48 heads have no directly identified automation mechanism, which does not prove human authorship. Build tests, dependency files, repeated short messages and repositories about test automation are not sufficient evidence on their own. The labels apply to inspected activity, not to whether an account holder is a ``bot.''

Removing only the 113 directly evidenced automated-head pushes reduces primary events from 237 to 124, accounts with any remaining event from 40 to 29, and active user-days from 87 to 48. The corresponding daily rate falls from 0.212 to 0.117 percent (\cref{tab:evidence_audit}). All 57 non-push primary events remain, including branch and pull-request events that may be related to automatic updates. This conservative decomposition is neither an estimate of human coding nor a corrected national outcome: inspecting a push head does not classify every commit in that push, and the audit does not cover all pre-period or donor activity. The national estimate is therefore left unchanged.

Automation also qualifies the earlier characterization of unusually active survivors. The 18 accounts with directly evidenced automated heads were active on 52.35 percent of pre-period days and made a median 79.5 pushes. The other 22 core publishers were active on 24.01 percent of days, with a median 20.5 pushes, compared with 17.18 percent and 21 pushes among other eligible accounts (\cref{tab:evidence_features}). Much of the pooled intensity difference is thus concentrated in accounts that also generate automated publication. It cannot be interpreted simply as greater human resilience or professional experience.

The strict author-equals-pusher restriction removes many machine-attributed commits but does not eliminate automation. One of the 1,097 onset-cohort commits has a matching account author yet a GitHub Actions committer and an exact historical workflow that converts a bibliography into generated webpages. Another account contributes ten daily README duration updates without changes in the stated number of solved exercises; these are not new source-code changes, although an unattended publisher was not recovered. Keeping the original 1,097 visible, excluding the directly documented generated commit gives 1,096; additionally excluding those ten duration-only commits gives 1,086. Neither sensitivity estimates total human work.

Repository content offers a more defensible characterization than inferred occupations. An explicit learning/exercise marker in a repository name is present for 83 of the 250 onset authors (33.2 percent), covering 87 repositories and 384 of their 1,097 commits (35.0 percent). Examples include assignments, data-structure exercises, programming practice and bootcamp repositories. These conservative markers are documented case by case; generic fitness or commercial-looking demo sites are not automatically counted as coursework. The markers describe published projects, not whether the authors are students or professionals. Contemporary reporting also describes software firms sending developers abroad during the blackout \citep{moli2024}; this establishes a possible channel of continued access, not the location of any sampled account.
